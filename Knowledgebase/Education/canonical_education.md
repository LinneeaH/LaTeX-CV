# Education

## Bachelor of Engineering (BEng) — Healthcare Technology (Sundhedsteknologi)

**Technical University of Denmark (DTU)**  
**September 2017 – January 2021**

### Educational focus and working model

The programme was built around full use-case, team-based engineering projects rather than isolated technical exercises.

Each semester concluded with an interdisciplinary project integrating the knowledge and methods from that semester’s courses. For example, the first-semester project combined concepts from Information Technology 1, Healthcare Technology 1, and Health Sciences 1. Across semesters, projects followed an end-to-end model resembling real healthcare technology development:

- Understanding user and clinical needs from a problem description
- Gathering and refining requirements
- Designing solution concepts and user flows
- Validating ideas with users or domain experts
- Iterating based on feedback
- Implementing, testing, and validating solutions
- Documenting and presenting results

Projects were typically completed in teams of four, requiring adaptability rather than strict role separation. Due to team composition, responsibility often included the most technical components, such as programming, system architecture, and data handling, while maintaining involvement in clinical context, physiology, requirements, UX, and overall solution design.

UX and UI design were treated as integral parts of system design rather than add-ons. Typical activities included:

- Creating paper mock-ups and early concepts for validation before implementation
- Adjusting designs following structured user or domain feedback
- Considering usability alongside technical implementation
- Using UML diagrams to describe systems and workflows
- Using Gantt charts, milestones, and project risk assessments to manage complexity
- Producing technical and project documentation

This project structure developed an early habit of designing systems for people, including when working on technically demanding components.

### Medical device development and regulatory thinking

A defining course simulated the full lifecycle of medical device development, including:

- Direct user-needs elicitation, including dialogue with a person living with Type 1 diabetes
- Requirements engineering
- Risk management and hazard analysis
- Regulatory and compliance considerations
- Verification and validation
- Consideration of real-world use, misuse, and edge cases

The course reinforced that practical constraints, user behaviour, and risk exposure fundamentally shape viable solutions. Technical novelty and practical usefulness were not treated as equivalent. Responsibility toward users and patients was presented as inseparable from technical decision-making.

### Bachelor Project

The bachelor project originated from work at the Functional Imaging Unit (FIU) at Glostrup Hospital, where renal MRI data from clinical research studies was analysed.

During data analysis it became clear how fragile the analysis workflow was. Although multiple researchers followed the same written instructions, kidney segmentations and annotations still differed between analysts. Small differences in interpretation, attention, experience, and workflow propagated into the final calculations, introducing analyst-to-analyst variability that was difficult to quantify.

Additional sources of variability originated from the software itself. Existing co-registration methods did not perform equally well across different kidney shapes or image sequences, further increasing uncertainty in measurements used for clinical research.

The project therefore aimed not only to automate manual work, but to investigate how segmentation and co-registration methods could reduce analyst-to-analyst variability, improve reproducibility, and strengthen confidence in downstream quantitative analysis.

The project investigated:

- Deep-learning-based kidney segmentation
- Image co-registration methods
- Medical image analysis
- MRI data preprocessing
- Validation of analytical methods
- Comparison of registration algorithms
- Sources of analytical uncertainty
- Analyst-to-analyst variability
- Reproducibility of image analysis
- Scientific reporting

The project also highlighted a broader engineering lesson that influenced subsequent academic choices.

Reliable data analysis depends not only on good algorithms, but on understanding every source of variability throughout the analytical pipeline. Analyst interpretation, software assumptions, data quality, acquisition protocols, and preprocessing choices all contribute to the reliability of downstream results.

This experience fundamentally shaped subsequent academic choices, leading to a deliberate focus during the MSc on machine learning, signal processing, statistical design of experiments, process optimisation, automation, and data-driven engineering. Automation became interesting not primarily as a way to reduce manual work, but as a means of improving consistency, reducing uncontrolled variation, and producing more trustworthy analyses.

The resulting segmentation and co-registration approach was designed for integration into the department's analysis workflow to reduce manual effort while improving consistency across analyses.

## Master of Science in Engineering (MSc) — Biomedical Engineering (Medicin og Teknologi)

**Technical University of Denmark (DTU)**  
**February 2021 – July 2023**

### Educational focus

The MSc deepened technical specialisation while preserving the same use-case-driven and interdisciplinary mindset.

The selected academic direction focused on domains where complexity increases and technical mastery alone is insufficient:

- Advanced signal processing in biomedical contexts
- Medical image analysis and machine learning
- Statistical design and analysis of experiments
- Auditory systems, perception, and hearing science
- Biomedical product development
- Scientific software and analytical workflows

Machine learning and data-analysis electives were deliberately prioritised after the clinical experience at Glostrup Hospital demonstrated that:

- Subtle errors and variability can propagate unnoticed in medical data
- Human variation in annotation and processing can affect analytical reliability
- Better analytical tools can expose hidden structure, uncertainty, and opportunities for improvement
- Automation can support quality and reproducibility rather than merely speed

The motivation for this direction was not algorithmic novelty for its own sake, but deeper insight and more robust data-driven decision-making.

### Audiology as a deliberate choice of complexity

Audiology was chosen because it represents a domain where:

- Perception, context, and variability dominate
- The limits of purely technical solutions become explicit
- Biological systems resist clean abstraction
- Signal manipulation and human experience may not align

Earlier work with ECG signals and medical imaging often involved limitations that were primarily technical or operational. Auditory systems introduced perceptual, physiological, and contextual boundaries that could not be bypassed through computation alone.

This developed a lasting respect for:

- System complexity
- Hidden assumptions
- Biological variability
- The distinction between algorithmic plausibility and practical usefulness
- The limits of abstraction in biomedical engineering

### Special course — Deep Learning for Multi-dimensional Biomedical Image Segmentation

Independent research project titled:

**Tri- and Quad-planar Segmentation of Tubular Pancreas Structures using U-Nets and Live Imaging 3D Microscopy**

The project investigated whether multiple two-dimensional views of multidimensional microscopy data could improve segmentation of tubular pancreatic structures.

The data consisted of noisy live-imaging confocal microscopy from embryonic mouse pancreases, represented across three spatial dimensions and time.

The project included:

- Python
- PyTorch
- U-Net architectures
- Biomedical image segmentation
- Confocal microscopy data
- Three-dimensional and four-dimensional image reasoning
- Dataset inspection and restructuring
- TIFF, LSM, and PNG data formats
- Linux-based DTU cluster workflows
- GPU computing
- Virtual environments
- Unix job-submission scripts
- Data transfer using PuTTY and WinSCP
- Supplementary debugging in Google Colab
- Memory optimisation
- Data preprocessing
- Reflection padding
- Class imbalance analysis
- Patch-to-image matching
- Dataset-class implementation
- Model training and evaluation
- IoU, sensitivity, and specificity
- Threshold analysis
- Overfitting investigation
- Experimental comparison across spatial and temporal planes
- Scientific reporting
- Documentation of limitations and unresolved implementation issues

Three two-dimensional spatial models were trained on the XY, XZ, and YZ planes. Their predictions were aligned and combined into a three-channel representation used as input to an additional model.

The combined spatial approach improved segmentation performance compared with the individual spatial models. IoU increased from approximately 0.58–0.59 for the individual models to approximately 0.65 for the combined model, while sensitivity increased from approximately 0.69–0.71 to approximately 0.79.

The project also involved substantial debugging and investigation of unexpected learning behaviour. Several spatial-temporal models were implemented, but unresolved problems in the shared code prevented a defensible conclusion regarding the contribution of the temporal dimension. The final six-view model was therefore prepared conceptually but not trained.

Relevant professional formation from the project included:

- Working productively with imperfect and inconsistently structured research data
- Inspecting data shape, format, metadata, class distribution, and labelling before modelling
- Adapting implementation to memory and infrastructure constraints
- Distinguishing declining loss from meaningful learning
- Choosing validation measures appropriate for severe class imbalance
- Comparing model behaviour critically rather than relying on a single metric
- Documenting unresolved problems instead of overstating results
- Preparing datasets, scripts, and model structures for future work when full completion was not feasible
- Recognising that research can produce useful boundary-setting knowledge without producing a complete or novel solution

### Master’s thesis — Auditory Signal Processing, WS Audiology

The thesis was conducted in collaboration with WS Audiology R&D in Erlangen, Germany, at the interface between audiology research and signal processing.

The work was explicitly exploratory, emphasising learning, hypothesis testing, and critical evaluation of feasibility rather than product delivery. Work was carried out largely independently with weekly academic and industrial supervision.

#### Original research direction and reframing

The initial project explored biologically inspired signal-processing strategies aimed at reducing spectro-temporal masking in complex auditory environments relevant to hearing-aid users.

The original formulation drew on concepts from cochlear implant research and auditory neuroscience. Through literature review, technical investigation, and discussion with supervisors, it became clear that:

- Some biologically inspired strategies do not transfer directly from cochlear implants to hearing aids
- A perceptually well-defined problem such as speech in noise may conceal fundamental theoretical and perceptual limitations
- The original problem aligned closely with the long-standing cocktail-party problem in auditory scene analysis
- The problem formulation exceeded the feasible scope of a master’s thesis

The scope was therefore actively reframed rather than forcing an unjustified solution.

#### Final thesis focus

The final work designed and evaluated controlled auditory signal-processing experiments targeting spectro-temporal masking effects.

The thesis included:

- Auditory signal processing
- Spectral and temporal data transformations
- MATLAB-based prototyping pipelines
- Audio preprocessing and transformation
- Experimental design
- Parameter variation
- Controlled listening experiments
- Modification and use of a GUI-based OLSA speech-intelligibility test
- Pilot testing
- Work with normal-hearing participants as a controlled proxy population
- Statistical analysis in MATLAB and JMP
- Validation and critical interpretation
- Literature analysis
- Scientific communication
- Communication of negative or null findings

#### Engineering judgement and professional formation

The thesis strengthened the ability to recognise when a problem formulation itself is the primary challenge.

It also developed a clearer distinction between:

- Algorithmic plausibility and perceptual usefulness
- Biological inspiration and biological equivalence
- Computational feasibility and practical value
- Negative findings and failed work

The work reinforced that:

- Fine-grained auditory mechanics and biological adaptability impose constraints that signal processing alone cannot necessarily overcome
- Unaccounted factors and hidden assumptions may dominate outcomes more than visible algorithmic choices
- Null or negative results can establish useful boundaries and reduce future risk or misdirected effort
- Refining project scope is an important engineering decision rather than a failure
- Complex and uncomfortable conclusions must still be communicated clearly to academic and industrial stakeholders

The thesis was successfully defended despite non-trivial negative findings, with explicit recognition from industrial supervision of the analytical depth and effort involved.

## Overall professional approach shaped by the education

Across both degrees, the education repeatedly reinforced the importance of:

- Understanding the current situation before proposing solutions
- Understanding users, clinical context, and operational constraints
- Investigating assumptions and limitations before implementation
- Treating UX, documentation, risk, and validation as core engineering concerns
- Anticipating variability, uncertainty, and error propagation in data-driven systems
- Balancing technical depth with responsibility toward users and patients
- Combining technical, scientific, human, and organisational perspectives
- Distinguishing practical usefulness from technical novelty
- Communicating limitations and trade-offs honestly
- Designing for reliable real-world use rather than idealised workflows

The education supports a professional profile that combines engineering, data, healthcare, scientific reasoning, and user-centred problem solving. It should not be interpreted as preparation for narrow implementation work alone.
